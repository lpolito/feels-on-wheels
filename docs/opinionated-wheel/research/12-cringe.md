# Cringe: do people say it about themselves?

(2026-11-22. One agent researched; a second, cold agent re-ran every count and read the sources, confirming the figures and correcting two claims, marked below. The handoff asked for evidence that people say "cringe" about their own feeling, since question 1 lists "that's cringe" as describing a thing.)

Counts are Google Books, English, the median of 2018–2022, per billion. Books are mostly narration, so "I cringed" there sometimes means flinching from fear or pain. Contractions ("I'm cringing", "that's cringe") can't be counted.

## Findings

1. **People say it about themselves, as a verb.** "I cringed" 293, "I cringe" 181, "made me cringe" 44, "makes me cringe" 34, "I was cringing" 3.6. Tagged, the verb is 678 against the adjective's 21. Describing a thing is rarer in books: cringeworthy 31, cringey 12 (rising sixfold over the years), "is cringe" 1.6. "Feel cringe" is about 0. Wiktionary tags the adjective "slang, derogatory", describing the thing; its figurative verb sense is "an inward feeling of disgust, embarrassment, or fear". A thesis on Discord found the adjective and noun dominate there, so online the "that's cringe" sense is probably common. Solid for books; unmeasured for speech and the web.
2. **It means both secondhand embarrassment and your own past acts, and the evidence can't rank them.** Your own: "still cringe" 7.1, "cringe to think" 5.0, "cringe when I think" 4.6, "cringe at the memory" 2.1; "cringe attacks" over old memories are widely written about. Someone else's: "cringed at his" 17.3, "cringed at her" 17.2, some of it disgust. "Cringe for him/her" is almost never written (0.2–0.4); people write "embarrassed for her" (16.3).
3. **Cringe carries judgment.** Escoe, Martin, and Salerno (Journal of Marketing Research, 2025, doi:10.1177/00222437241305104) call it "a distinct form of vicarious embarrassment", tied to acts the watcher finds inexcusable, with a very negative view of the person. A 2026 preprint (Deutchman and Lahiri, PsyArXiv) finds less empathy and more judgment than in vicarious embarrassment. A 2022 European Psychiatry abstract, "Cringe or Vicarious Embarrassment", describes Gen Z's use as a response to "embarrassment or social awkwardness by proxy". The research on vicarious embarrassment itself (Krach et al. 2011; Paulus et al. 2013) never uses the word. *Corrected by the checker:* the paper doesn't say "disdain", and "personal cringe" at your own past acts is from the authors' interview, not the paper.
4. **Labels.** "Embarrassed for them" 4.6 (her 16, him 15) is the phrase for the kind, empathic feeling. "Secondhand embarrassment" is 2.7, rising fast, but long. The cringe verb family dwarfs both. "Cringing" fits "I'm cringing" better than "Cringe" fits "I feel ___", but neither reads naturally after "I feel"; Cringe reads as a noun beside Dread and Regret.

## What it means for the wheel

- Cringe passes question 5: people say it about their own feeling, mostly as "I cringed" and "makes me cringe".
- Its definition ("someone else did something embarrassing, and you feel embarrassed for them") describes the empathic kind, the one people call "embarrassed for her", and leaves out cringing at your own past. The word covers wincing at something embarrassing, someone else's or your own remembered act.
