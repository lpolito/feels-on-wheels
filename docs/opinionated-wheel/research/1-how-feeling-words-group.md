# Report 1: research check on how feeling words are structured

(Written by a fresh agent asked to verify or refute a draft's claims against primary sources.)

I couldn't reach several primary full texts. PMC began blocking fetches partway through. Ekman's 1999 chapter is a scan with no text layer. Parrott (2001) and Storm and Storm (1987) are paywalled. Where I fell back on abstracts, secondary sources, or my own background knowledge, I say so below.

## Claims the draft made

1. The seven-core wheel (Roberts 2015: Happy, Sad, Fearful, Angry, Surprised, Disgusted, Bad) takes six of its cores from Ekman's basic emotions, which Ekman chose because each has a distinct, cross-culturally recognized facial expression.
2. Shaver, Schwartz, Kirson, and O'Connor (1987) had people sort 135 emotion words; the basic-level families were love, joy, anger, sadness, and fear, with surprise as a small extra; disgust and envy fall under anger, and shame, guilt, and loneliness under sadness.
3. Cowen and Keltner (2017) found 27 categories joined by gradients, supporting "a hierarchy is a convenience; put each word where people would look".
4. Russell's circumplex; Jackson et al. (2019) found emotion concepts across 2,474 languages are differentiated mainly by valence and activation; supporting a rule that a nested word shares its parent's valence and roughly its arousal.
5. Silvia's knowledge emotions (surprise, interest, confusion, awe) as one family on a wheel.
6. (Added) What basic-emotion and constructionist views each imply for a wheel.

## 1. Roberts's wheel and Ekman's faces: partly right

- **Roberts's cores.** Six of Roberts's seven cores do line up with Ekman's six. But secondary sources trace the wheel to Gloria Willcox's 1982 Feeling Wheel by way of Kaitlin Robb's 2014 version, not to Ekman. Nothing found in which Roberts says he drew on Ekman, so "takes six from Ekman" describes an overlap, not a documented source. "Bad" fits no research list.
- **Why Ekman's list is six.** The six came from Tomkins's affect theory, and they are the emotions that Ekman's posed-photo recognition studies (1969 to 1972) supported across cultures. But the theory never defined a basic emotion by its face alone. Ekman (1992) gives several characteristics, of which a distinctive universal signal is only one.
  - That paper already names contempt, shame, guilt, embarrassment, and awe as possible additions.
  - The 1999 chapter lists about 15 (from memory; scan unreadable).
  - Ekman and Cordaro (2011) and Ekman's own site settle on seven, adding contempt.
  - Keltner, Sauter, Tracy, and Cowen (2019) report more than 20 emotions with distinct expressive patterns.
- **Counter-evidence on faces.** Barrett and colleagues (2019, PSPI) find facial movements are not reliable or specific signs of these emotions; people scowl in only about 25% of angry moments. Russell (1994) faulted the forced-choice method behind the recognition studies (from background knowledge).
- **Is the face-based origin a fair criticism?** Yes, though a different point makes it more strongly. Being recognizable on a face is a test of signaling, not of how useful a word is for self-report. Love is a top-level family in people's own sorting of feeling words and has no single distinctive face. In Ekman's favor: he described each basic emotion as a *family* of related states, which suits a design with cores and nested words.

## 2. Shaver and colleagues (1987): confirmed, with nuance

- **Word count and method** (confirmed through the Basque replication's description, plus secondary sources): 112 students rated 213 emotion names for prototypicality. The top 135 went on cards, a separate 100 students sorted them, analyzed by hierarchical cluster analysis plus multidimensional scaling.
- **Families.** Above the basic level, positive versus negative. Basic level: love, joy, anger, sadness, fear, plus "a very small surprise category" on the positive side.
- **Placements.** All check out: disgust (with revulsion and contempt) under anger, envy (with jealousy) under anger; guilt, shame, and loneliness under sadness, loneliness in a neglect/rejection group.
- **Parrott (2001)** reformats this same tree; not independent evidence. Not reached directly.
- **Replications.**
  - Shaver, Wu, and Schwartz (1992): US, Italy, China (not Indonesia). In China, love was not a separate basic-level family, and shame and "sad love" appeared as their own negative families.
  - Indonesia: Shaver, Murdaya, and Fraley (2001). Same five families, no surprise group.
  - Basque (2006): the five families plus five small positive groups; surprise words often sorted with fear words. In all three of these languages, disgust and contempt fell under anger.
- **Storm and Storm (1987)**, "A taxonomic study of the vocabulary of emotions": sorted English emotion words, refined with expert judges. Findings not verified.

## 3. Cowen and Keltner (2017): finding confirmed, design inference only partly supported

- 853 US participants each rated 30 of about 2,000 film clips; 27 kinds of reported experience joined by gradients (anxiety to fear to horror to disgust).
- **Barrett, Khan, Dy, and Brooks (2018):** analysis was confirmatory (yes/no on a fixed list of 34 words); ratings averaged per clip hide individual differences; within-category variation more consistent with constructionism.
- **Reply:** the method was unsupervised; valence and arousal don't explain the categories; experience is "neither simply clustered nor uniform".
- **Strength of critique:** moderate. Both sides accept fuzzy, graded boundaries; they disagree about what categories are.
- **The draft's inference:** fuzzy boundaries supported. "A hierarchy is a convenience; put each word where people look" isn't tested by this work: it's about experiences elicited by clips, not looking up words, and its 27 dimensions don't nest. Better support for "where people would look" is the sorting data in claim 2.

## 4. Valence, arousal, and Fontaine: summary confirmed, nesting rule partly unsupported

- **Jackson et al. (2019, Science 366:1517)** (confirmed via press release, HRAF record, ANU repository):
  - Colexification across 2,474 languages shows emotion meanings varying *more* than color terms, with similarity tracking geography.
  - Valence and activation were the strongest predictors: valence first overall and in 13 of 19 families, activation in the other 6.
  - Families differ in detail: "anxiety" goes with fear in Tai-Kadai languages but with grief and regret in Austroasiatic ones.
  - "Mainly by valence and activation" is accurate; leaving out the large cultural variation is not.
- **Fontaine, Scherer, Roesch, and Ellsworth (2007):** four dimensions in order: evaluation-pleasantness, potency-control, activation-arousal, unpredictability (novelty). From 144 features across six components, three languages. Abstract only.
- **The nesting rule:**
  - Sharing the parent's valence: supported. Valence is the first split in every sorting study and every colexification network.
  - Sharing roughly the parent's arousal: not supported. People's own trees span arousal within a family (irritation and rage both under anger; contentment and euphoria under joy). Fontaine shows what separates anger from fear is power, not arousal; surprise is defined by novelty.
  - Surprise is the standing exception to valence: it sits near the positive-negative divide.

## 5. Silvia's knowledge emotions: grouping confirmed, family-on-a-wheel only partly sound

- Silvia's Noba module names surprise, interest, confusion, and awe, which arise "when something violates what people expected or believed". Keltner and Shiota (2003) may have coined the label (unverified).
- **Catch:** the grouping is by appraisal and function, not by how the feelings feel. Confusion is unpleasant; interest and awe mostly pleasant. Epistemic-emotion work (Vogl, Pekrun, et al. 2019) likewise groups by what the emotion is about, not valence. As one family under a core, it breaks the valence rule.

## 6. Basic-emotion versus constructionist views

- **Basic-emotion view:** a few families, each with variants (Ekman's family idea). But the modern basic-emotion school (Keltner, Cowen) argues for 20+ categories.
- **Constructionist view (Barrett):** cores are folk concepts, not natural kinds; each word covers varied instances; learning finer words helps regulation (Kashdan, Barrett, McKnight 2015).
- **Common ground** both sides likely accept:
  - Emotion words are prototype categories with fuzzy edges.
  - Valence is the dominant dimension of word meaning (Lindquist, a constructionist, is senior author on Jackson).
  - Naming feelings precisely helps; affect labeling dampens emotional responses (Torre and Lieberman 2018).
  - The English lexicon's details don't carry over to every language.
- **Defensible framing:** the wheel is a map of how English speakers group feeling words, not a claim about biological emotion kinds.

## What a wheel designer can safely rely on (most to least solid)

1. Split by valence first. Every sorting study and the 2,474-language data agree.
2. People's own groupings of English words have a stable, replicated shape: love, joy, anger, sadness, fear at the basic level, surprise small and ambiguous. Disgust and envy sort under anger; shame, guilt, and loneliness under sadness. Replicates in English, Indonesian, Basque. Evidence for "put each word where people would look".
3. Boundaries are fuzzy; some words could sit under two parents.
4. More, finer words help users (granularity, affect labeling).
5. Ekman's six are a face-signaling list, not a self-report taxonomy. Leaving out love, and including disgust as a core, goes against how people sort words.
6. Weak: matching arousal under a parent, and treating knowledge emotions as one family.
7. Don't claim universality; structure differs across cultures (China, Jackson).
