# Report 2: research check on naming feelings

(Written by a fresh agent asked to verify or refute a draft's claims against primary sources.)

**Access notes.** PMC pages sat behind a captcha, so PMC articles were read through Europe PMC's full-text feed. The two Ortony/Clore/Foss 1987 papers are scans; OCR'd and read directly. Not reached: Lieberman 2007, Torre & Lieberman 2018, Shaver et al. 1987, Tangney et al. 1996, Eastwood et al. 2012, Cowen & Keltner 2017, Kashdan et al. 2015, Willcox 1982.

## Claims the draft made

1. People who differentiate unpleasant feelings (granularity) cope better.
2. Labeling a feeling dampens the brain's alarm response (Lieberman 2007).
3. Tangney: shame, guilt, and embarrassment are distinct; shame is about the self, guilt about a behavior; shame pulls toward hiding, guilt toward amends; embarrassment is lighter and shorter.
4. NVC's "faux feelings" (abandoned, betrayed, rejected, disrespected) describe what someone did, not a feeling.
5. Kama muta ("being moved") is a distinct emotion.
6. (Asked) Are boredom, stress, tiredness, and busy emotions?
7. (Asked) Is there evidence feelings wheels help?

## 1. Granularity helps coping: partly right
- Mostly correlational, small effects. Seah & Coifman meta-analysis (Emotion, 17 studies): r = −.15 between negative differentiation and harmful behaviors (binge drinking, self-injury); r = −.09 after controlling for overall negative feeling. O'Toole et al. 2020: r ≈ .06–.15 across 27 studies.
- Interventions: Vedernikova, Kuppens & Erbas 2021 (n = 120): five days teaching what 12 emotions mean raised negative differentiation vs control, held at one month; no difference for pleasant; well-being results "very inconsistent". Matt, Seah & Coifman 2024 (n = 118, emotion-word training): "no direct effects of intervention group" on differentiation, self-efficacy, or distress; only an indirect effect among engaged participants.
- Unpleasant vs pleasant: more consistent for unpleasant, but most studies only measure unpleasant, so weak support.
- Counter-evidence: Vine, Boyd & Pennebaker 2020: people who naturally use more negative-feeling words report more distress. Hoemann et al. 2021: repeated mood sampling alone raises measured granularity. RULER's trials improve classroom climate, but vocabulary can't be separated from the program.
- URLs: https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/emotion-differentiation-and-behavioral-dysregulation-clinical-and-nonclinical-samples-meta · https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.703757/full · https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0299540 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7483527/ · https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.704125/full · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11121-012-0305-2

## 2. Labeling a feeling dampens it: partly right
- Lieberman et al. 2011, four studies: labeling reduced self-reported distress every time, modestly (6.64 vs 6.25 on a 9-point scale in Study 1). Also reduced pleasure from pleasant images. People predicted labeling would make them feel worse.
- Caveat: classic studies attach provided labels to pictures; labels describe the picture, not the person's own feeling.
- Specific vs broad labels: no direct test found. Kircanski et al. 2012: spider-fearful people who used more fear/anxiety words during exposure had larger drops in fear (quantity, not precision). Nook, Satpute & Ochsner 2021: naming one's own emotion made later reappraisal and acceptance work less well ("crystallize").
- Choice overload: no emotion-specific study. Scheibehenne et al. 2010: average effect near zero. Chernev et al. 2015: appears when choices are complex and preferences uncertain.
- No failed replication found, no independent large replication; mostly one lab.
- URLs: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3444304 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9383041/ · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22902568/ · https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706 · https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf · https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual

## 3. Shame, guilt, embarrassment: partly right
- Confirmed by Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek 2007 (read in full): shame about the self, guilt about a behavior (Lewis 1971). Shame goes with trying to "deny, hide, or escape"; guilt with repair. Embarrassment less about morality, from minor public mishaps.
- "Lighter and shorter" rests on Tangney et al. 1996 (textbook summary only): embarrassment the least intense and shortest of the three.
- Critiques: Leach & Cidam 2015 meta-analysis (90 samples): shame goes with constructive approach when the failure is fixable (g = .47) and withdrawal when it isn't (g = −.34), so "shame pulls toward hiding" is too broad. Montes Sánchez 2014: boundary blurrier than questionnaires suggest. Tracy & Robins 2006 refine: shame with stable, uncontrollable causes; guilt with changeable, controllable ones. Cultural work secondary only; GRID summary says the two are told apart similarly across cultures, with differences linked to power distance.
- Sorting studies: Shaver et al. 1987 (via Parrott's table): shame and guilt form a subgroup under sadness; embarrassment and humiliation in a "neglect" subgroup, also under sadness. None group with fear. Ortony et al. 1987 class ashamed, embarrassed, and guilt as emotions, but "guilty" as an outside fact (found guilty).
- URLs: https://w.astro.berkeley.edu/~kalas/documents/ethics/annurev.psych.56.091103.070145.pdf · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26641074/ · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114200/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification · https://psu.pb.unizin.org/psych425/chapter/subjective-feelings-5/

## 4. Faux feelings: partly right
NVC itself has no research behind it, but Ortony, Clore & Foss give the distinction strong academic grounding.
- "Being angry is an emotion, but being abandoned is not." "Feel abandoned" reports the cause of the feeling, which might be anger, fear, hurt, or sadness.
- Category "External conditions: Objective Descriptions": abandoned, cheated, insulted, neglected, ignored; also alone, isolated, helpless, safe. "Lonely" and "hurt" count as emotions.
- Test (Clore et al.): 585 words rated in "feeling ___" and "being ___" frames. True emotion words scored high in both; outside-condition words rated as emotions only after "feeling", and the frames differed about twice as much for them.
- Gaps: betrayed, rejected, disrespected, manipulated aren't in their list; same category is the agent's inference.
- Counter-evidence: people experience these as emotional (the authors concede "feeling abandoned" refers to some emotional state); in lay sorting, rejection and insult fall under sadness; Leary et al. 1998 treat hurt feelings from rejection as a real emotion.
- URLs: https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~ortony/Andrew_Ortony_files/1987%20-%20Affective%20lexicon-structure.pdf · https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~ortony/Andrew_Ortony_files/1987%20-%20Affective%20lexicon-foundations.pdf · https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-causes-phenomenology-and-consequences-of-hurt-feelings/

## 5. Kama muta: partly right
- Zickfeld et al. 2019: 3,542 participants, 19 countries, 15 languages; appraisal, valence, sensations, motivation, and labels highly correlated in every sample.
- Authors' limits: mostly European students; self-report only; scripted videos set to music; no causal evidence for the sudden-closeness account.
- Debate: Menninghaus et al. 2015 treat being moved as a mixed emotion; Cova & Deonna offer a rival account. Nearly all evidence from one group.
- Ortony's list classes "moved" and "touched" as emotions.
- URLs: https://www.openu.ac.il/Lists/MediaServer_Documents/PersonalSites/DrRavitNussinson/Zickfeld%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20KAVIAR%20Project.pdf · https://duo.uio.no/bitstream/10852/71491/2/Moving+Through+The+Literature+PrePrint.pdf

## 6. Boredom, stress, tiredness, busy
- Boredom: confirmed as an emotion (Danckert, Mugon, Struk & Eastwood 2018: "ubiquitous and consequential human emotion"). Ortony classes "bored" as a cognitive state.
- Stress: not one emotion. Lazarus 1993: knowing someone feels angry, anxious, guilty, or sad "tells us much more" than knowing they're stressed.
- Tiredness: a body state. Ortony classes tired, exhausted, sleepy as physical states; Clore et al. found body-state words got the lowest emotion ratings.
- Busy: no research treats it as a feeling.
- URLs: https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Danckert2018.pdf · https://commonweb.unifr.ch/artsdean/pub/gestens/f/as/files/4660/18222_105619.pdf

## 7. Evidence that feelings wheels help: none found
- Willcox 1982 is a three-page article presenting the tool, not a study.
- No controlled or outcome study of any feelings wheel found. A clinical resource site (Headway) says emotion worksheets are "not evidence-based".
- Closest: RULER's Feeling Words Curriculum quasi-experiment (273 students): better grades and teacher ratings, as part of a whole program; the two small vocabulary trials above.
- URLs: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215378201200411 · https://headway.co/resources/emotion-identification-worksheet · https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps12-007

## What a wheel designer can safely rely on (most to least solid)
1. Words like abandoned or cheated name what happened, not the feeling (tested primary taxonomy). People still find them meaningful, so link each to the feelings it causes rather than drop it.
2. Stress is an umbrella; tiredness is a body state.
3. Boredom, and being moved or touched, are treated as emotions in mainstream research. Kama muta as distinct rests on one group.
4. Naming a feeling modestly lowers its intensity, for pleasant feelings too. Replicated mostly by one lab; small effects. Naming may make a feeling harder to reframe right afterward.
5. Shame and guilt are distinguishable, embarrassment milder. Don't claim shame always drives hiding. Lay sorting puts all three near sadness, not fear. "Guilty" is ambiguous.
6. Telling unpleasant feelings apart goes with somewhat better coping. Correlational, small. Brief training can raise it, inconsistently.
7. Not supported: that wheels themselves help; that more specific words help more than broad ones; that bigger lists cause choice overload.
