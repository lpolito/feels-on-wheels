# Where disgust belongs

(Written on 2026-11-21 by a fresh research agent, saved here by the main session because the agent couldn't write files. Asked after Lucas questioned disgust under anger: "I am blown away that people would put disgust under angry." It was also asked his question: someone feeling disgust starts at the center; would they tap Angry? Its scripts and a crop of the Basque dendrogram were in the session scratchpad. The key claims were then checked by a second agent; see the note at the end.)

## Bottom line

- **Disgust under Angry:** a moderate case, but only for the moral, everyday sense of "disgusted". The sorting studies back it more weakly than claimed. Only English has a disgust group inside anger. In Basque the disgust words are mixed in with hate, and Indonesian sorted no word for physical disgust at all.
- **Disgust as its own family:** strong for physical disgust. Memories of being "grossed out" are almost never about anger, and it differs from anger in the body, the face, and what people want to do.
- **Split:** the best fit to the evidence. Moral "disgust" runs together with anger in self-reports (r ≈ .8); bodily disgust doesn't.
- **From the center:** someone grossed out would probably not tap Angry. Someone morally disgusted plausibly would, since they also feel angry. Card sorts measure how similar the words seem; they say little about which core someone who feels it would tap.

## What each source found

**Shaver et al. 1987 (English).** Full text not reached (paywall; ResearchGate, Academia, and Scribd blocked), so the join heights in its Figure 1 weren't read.
- From Alonso-Arbiol 2006, Table 1 (full text): of the 135 words, the anger family's subgroup 4 is "disgust, revulsion, contempt". Loathing and scorn sit in subgroup 3 with rage and hate. The anger family has 6 groups and 29 words; the others are irritation, exasperation, rage, envy and jealousy, and torment.
- Shaver p. 1069, as quoted by van der Eijk and Columbus 2023 (full text, secondary): "in the adult emotion lexicon it [disgust] becomes metaphorically transformed into a type of anger akin to contempt."
- No word for physical disgust (nauseated, grossed out) was among the 135: only words rated as typical emotions were sorted.
- Edelstein and Shaver 2007 (full text): in word studies, disgust gets "lower ratings of emotion-prototypicality", is slower to be recognized, is rarely listed when people name emotions, and tends "to appear as subordinate categories".

**Indonesian (Shaver et al. 2001),** from Alonso-Arbiol Table 2 (full text, all 124 words). The only disgust-related word is *muak*, in the anger subgroup "(6. hatred, loathing) muak, benci [hate], dendam [grudge]". *Jijik*, the everyday word for physical disgust, isn't among the 124. The official dictionary (KBBI) gives *muak* three senses: fed up from having something too often; disgusted to the point of wanting to vomit; bored or disgusted by what one sees or hears.

**Basque (Alonso-Arbiol et al. 2006),** full text, including the dendrogram. 102 people sorted 124 words, clustered by average linkage; the tree is cut at 60, and a join at height h means roughly 102 − h of the 102 sorters put the two groups together.
- *Higuina* (glossed "revulsion, disgust, repugnance; hatred, loathing"; unfamiliar to 14%) and *nazka* ("disgust, repugnance, revulsion") have no group of their own. They sit inside a 12-word "(9. hatred, aversion)" group with *gorrotoa* (hate), *antipatia*, and two words for contempt.
- That group joins the rage group at about 73 (roughly 29 of 102 sorters put them together). Envy, jealousy, and distrust join later, at about 79. The anger family joins sadness and fear at about 95. The family is labeled "hatred/anger".
- *Disgustua* (a Spanish loan meaning "displeasure") went to sadness.
- The authors: "In all three countries, being disgusted or contemptuous is viewed as a form of anger."
- Surprise formed its own family of 2 words in Basque and 3 in English, so the method could produce tiny families; disgust didn't form one.

**Shaver, Wu, and Schwartz 1992 (US, Italy, China):** not found; the Basque paper's summary of it doesn't mention disgust.

**Russell and Fehr 1994** (abstract, and Russell and Giner-Sorolla's description): disgust "appeared to be a subcategory of anger" across free listing, typicality ratings, and reaction times.

**GRID (Fontaine et al. 2021,** full text): disgust fits the four-dimension model poorly (R² .67, against anger .92, fear .88, hate .78). Nothing on how close disgust is to anger.

**Cowen and Keltner 2017** (abstract only): disgust is one of 27 distinct categories, on a gradient "from anxiety to fear to horror to disgust". It shades toward horror and fear, not anger.

**Jackson et al. 2019** (full text and its 24-concept list): disgust isn't one of the 24 concepts, so the study says nothing about it.

**Nabi 2002** (abstract; tables as reproduced in a CC-licensed Penn State open textbook). People recalled a time they felt a given word:

| Word recalled | Stories with anger themes | With disgust themes | "Feel like throwing up" (1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anger | 100% | 0% | 2.19 |
| Disgust | 75% | 25% | 3.61 |
| Disgusted | 74% | 26% | 3.52 |
| Repulsed | 44% | 56% | 3.97 |
| Grossed out | 8% | 92% | 3.96 |

Abstract: lay "disgust" reflects "irritation, or anger, as often as repulsion".

**Van der Eijk and Columbus 2023** (full text, N = 1,608): "I felt disgusted" and "I experienced disgust" load on both the anger and the disgust factors; "revolted" and "repulsed" load on disgust only. Expressions of moral disgust "reflect both disgust and anger".

**Gutierrez and Giner-Sorolla 2007** (full text): for taboo scenarios, anger words and disgust words correlate at r = .77 to .82; with face-based measures, r = .33. Harm predicts anger better, taboo predicts disgust better.

**Russell and Giner-Sorolla 2013** (full text): "disgust at nonbodily violations is often associated with anger and hard to separate from it, while bodily violations more consistently predict disgust independently of anger."

**Others** (abstracts unless noted):
- Royzman et al. 2014: violations with no contamination bring little nausea or gagging, but much anger. Royzman et al. 2008 (via Russell and Giner-Sorolla): sibling incest brought nausea more than anger; a photo of Hitler brought "disgust" tied to wanting to lash out, not nausea.
- Marzillier and Davey 2004: physical disgust triggers showed "no evidence of elevated ratings" on other negative emotions, anger included; moral and social ones scored high on sadness, contempt, fear, and anger.
- Piazza and Landy 2019 (full text): lay people link anger, not disgust, to moral disapproval.
- Molho et al. 2017; Tybur et al. 2020: for moral violations, disgust goes with indirect aggression and anger with direct; disgust rises when the victim is someone else, anger when it's oneself.
- Kollareth and Russell 2016: English "disgust" covers both unclean things and moral violations; its Hindi and Malayalam translations mean mainly moral violations.
- Ruba et al. 2017: "older children, and even adults, consistently misidentify disgust expressions as anger."

**Small World of Words** (association strengths):
- "disgust" gives physical words .42 in total (gross .069, vomit .040, yuck .033, sick .026, smell .022), hate and dislike .077, anger words .011, fear and horror .026, sad .007.
- "disgusting", "gross", and "repulsive" give no anger words; "anger", "angry", and "mad" never give a disgust word.
- The cues that most often give "disgusted" are horrified .040, put off .027, and aghast .026; outrage gives it at .007, furious at .003.

**Google Books (2018–22, per billion):**
- "disgusted with" 493, "by" 343, "at" 107. What follows "disgusted with" is mostly people or oneself (the 115, myself 42, himself 40, her 29, him 26, herself 25).
- "disgusted by the" + idea 5.8, sight 4.6, thought 4.3, way 4.0, smell 2.1.
- "shocked and disgusted" 10.4, "angry and disgusted" 8.6; "anger and disgust" 47, "fear and disgust" 38, "horror and disgust" 34, "shame and disgust" 18.
- Disgust at yourself ("disgusted with myself / himself / herself", about 107) is a third use, closer to shame.

## Where sources disagree

- Whether moral disgust is anger in other words (Nabi, Royzman 2014, Piazza and Landy) or something distinct (Molho, Tybur, and Russell and Giner-Sorolla for bodily violations). Van der Eijk and Columbus find both.
- Purity violations: the CAD triad says disgust; Royzman 2014 finds anger.
- What disgust is closest to: in sorting, anger and hate; in Cowen and Keltner, horror and fear; in everyday pairings, anger, fear, and horror about equally.

## What cuts against "people sorted disgust with anger in English, Indonesian, and Basque"

- Indonesian sorted no word for physical disgust; *muak* is mostly "fed up" or "sick of".
- In Basque and Indonesian, the disgust words sat with hate, not rage, and formed no group of their own. Only English has a separate disgust group.
- In all three, only words rated as typical emotions were sorted, which filtered out words for physical disgust, and the English trio includes contempt.
- No join heights could be verified for English.

## The check

A second agent checked ten load-bearing claims against the sources on 2026-11-21. All ten hold, with these corrections:
- **Gutierrez and Giner-Sorolla:** the .33 isn't anger words against face measures; it's the correlation between two composites, each averaging a word index with its face measure (Experiment 2). Experiment 3's composites correlated at .64, so quoting only .33 overstates how far faces separate anger from disgust.
- **Nabi 2002** was checked only through the textbook's reproduction of its tables (the paper is closed), and the abstract names the condition "revulsion" where the tables say "Repulsed". People recalling "disgust" or "disgusted" still rated "feel like throwing up" 3.5 to 3.6, against 2.19 for anger, so moral disgust carries some bodily feeling too.
- **Shaver et al. 1987's** p. 1069 quote is confirmed only as quoted by van der Eijk and Columbus.
- **Van der Eijk and Columbus** was read as the preprint; "repulsed" isn't named in the passage but follows from "only two of the generic disgust items loaded significantly on the anger factor".
- **Cowen and Keltner's** stimuli were videos, so its gradient speaks mainly to physical disgust; "not anger" is the report's inference.
- **Edelstein and Shaver's** remark covers disgust, surprise, and contempt together.
- **Alonso-Arbiol:** the Basque join heights measure 73.1, 79.0, and 95.2; *desprezioa* is glossed "disdain" and *mespretxua* "scorn, contempt".
